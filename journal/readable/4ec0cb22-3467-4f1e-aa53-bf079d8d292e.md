**Claude:** ## What `gillespie()` is

Your `gene_expression.py` uses ODEs: dm/dt = r − γm·m. They treat mRNA and protein as smooth, continuous amounts, so the curve rises and settles at exactly m\* = r/γm.

A real bacterium has whole molecules, often only a few mRNAs per gene. They're made and destroyed one at a time, at random moments. So the real counts jump around the ODE value instead of sitting on it. That randomness is called **gene expression noise**.

The **Gillespie algorithm** (Daniel Gillespie, 1976; note the spelling) simulates this exactly. It doesn't step time forward in fixed increments. It answers two questions over and over: **when** does the next reaction happen, and **which** reaction is it?

## The four reactions (`gillespie.py`)

| # | Reaction | Effect | Propensity (how likely per unit time) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Transcription | m → m + 1 | a₁ = r |
| 2 | mRNA decay | m → m − 1 | a₂ = γm · m |
| 3 | Translation | p → p + 1 | a₃ = β · m |
| 4 | Protein decay | p → p − 1 | a₄ = γp · p |

These come straight from your ODEs: each term in dm/dt and dp/dt becomes one reaction. The decay rates grow with the count, because more molecules means more of them can decay.

## The loop (`_simulate`, lines ~45–100)

Starting from m = 0, p = 0, t = 0, it repeats:

1. **Add up the propensities:** a_total = a₁ + a₂ + a₃ + a₄.
2. **Draw the waiting time** to the next reaction from an exponential distribution with rate a_total:
   `t_next = t − ln(1 − U) / a_total`, where U is a random number in [0, 1).
   When a lot is going on (large a_total), the wait is short.
3. **Pick which reaction happens**, weighted by propensity. Draw u uniformly in [0, a_total) and see which slice it lands in:
   ```
   |--- a1 ---|-- a2 --|---- a3 ----|- a4 -|
   0                                    a_total
   ```
   A reaction with twice the propensity is twice as likely to be chosen.
4. **Apply it** (m += 1, m −= 1, p += 1 or p −= 1) and set t = t_next.
5. Stop when t reaches t_max.

**Example** with r = 1, γm = 0.5, β = 1, γp = 0.5, starting at m = 2, p = 3:
- a₁ = 1, a₂ = 1, a₃ = 2, a₄ = 1.5, so a_total = 5.5. The average wait is 1/5.5 ≈ 0.18 time units.
- Translation has a 2/5.5 ≈ 36% chance of being next, and transcription has 1/5.5 ≈ 18%.

## The two functions you call

**`gillespie(r, γm, β, γp, t_max, ...)`** runs one trajectory and returns `(times, ms, ps)`, sampled at `n_points` evenly spaced times. You use it to **plot** a noisy path. Because the counts are step functions, each grid time records the state the cell was in at that moment.

**`steady_state_stats(...)`** runs one long trajectory and returns the mean, variance and **Fano factor** (variance ÷ mean) of m and p. Two details matter here:
- **Time weighting:** a state that lasted 2 time units counts twice as much as one that lasted 1. Averaging the reaction events equally would give the wrong answer.
- **`t_burn`:** the start is skipped, because the system begins at 0 and takes time to reach steady state.

The **`autorepression=(K, n, r_basal)`** option makes the protein repress its own gene. Transcription then becomes a₁ = r_basal + (r − r_basal)·Kⁿ/(Kⁿ + pⁿ), which is your Level 1 repressor formula with x = p. This is the one case where r changes during a run.

## How you know it's correct

For constant r, theory gives exact answers, and the tests (`test_gillespie.py`) check the simulation against them:

| | Mean | Fano factor |
|---|---|---|
| mRNA | r / γm (same as the ODE) | 1 (Poisson noise) |
| protein | rβ / (γm·γp) (same as the ODE) | 1 + β/(γm + γp) |

So the **mean** matches your ODE steady state, but the Gillespie simulation also gives the **noise**. Protein noise is above 1 because each mRNA produces a **burst** of proteins before it decays. The quantity β/(γm+γp) is roughly the burst size. With the example numbers, the Fano factor is 1 + 1/1 = 2, so proteins are twice as noisy as a Poisson process. The ODEs can't show this, and it's usually the point of running a Gillespie simulation.

You can see it yourself by running `python3 gillespie.py`. It prints the simulated mean and Fano factor next to the expected values.

**Student:** Explain the Gillispie Argorithim to me in plain language.

**Claude:** The Gillespie algorithm is a way to simulate a cell **one molecule at a time**, by repeatedly asking two questions: *when does the next thing happen*, and *what is it?*

## Why it's needed

Your equations say "mRNA settles at r/γm." That's like saying "a shop gets 10 customers an hour." It's true on average, but customers actually show up at random: three at once, then nobody for a while. A cell is the same. With only a handful of molecules, those random ups and downs are large enough to matter. The Gillespie algorithm shows the ups and downs, not just the average.

## How it works

The cell can do four things:
- make an mRNA
- destroy an mRNA
- make a protein from an mRNA
- destroy a protein

Each one happens at random, but some are more likely than others. The algorithm then repeats these steps:

1. **Check how busy the cell is.** Add up how likely each of the four events is right now. More molecules means more going on. For example, 10 mRNAs can make proteins faster than 1 can.

2. **Wait a random amount of time.** If the cell is busy, the wait is usually short. If it's quiet, the wait is usually long. It's like waiting for a bus: on a busy route the next one comes soon.

3. **Pick which event happens**, with more likely events picked more often. It's like a raffle: each event gets tickets in proportion to how likely it is, and you draw one.

4. **Update the counts.** Add or remove one mRNA or protein, move the clock forward, and go back to step 1.

After thousands of rounds, you have a jagged, realistic history of how many mRNAs and proteins the cell had over time.

## What you learn from it

- **The average** matches your equations. That's a good check that the simulation is right.
- **The noise**, meaning how much the counts jump around, is the new information. Proteins turn out to be noisier than mRNA. Each mRNA produces a **burst** of several proteins before it's destroyed, so proteins arrive in clumps rather than one at a time.

The equations give you the average. The Gillespie algorithm gives you the average plus the randomness around it, which is what a real cell actually shows.

# Part 1: How the two functions work

## `gillespie()`: one cell's story over time

Think of it as filming one cell. You give it the rates (how fast mRNA is made, how fast it breaks down, and so on) and how long to film (`t_max`). It plays the cell forward one random event at a time, using the "when's the next event, and which one is it?" loop.

A film is continuous, but you can only store frames. So `gillespie()` takes snapshots at evenly spaced times (`n_points` of them, 1001 by default) and writes down how many mRNAs and proteins the cell had at each moment.

It gives you back three lists of the same length:
- `times`: when each snapshot was taken, from 0 to `t_max`
- `ms`: the number of mRNAs in each snapshot
- `ps`: the number of proteins in each snapshot

**Use it when you want to plot** what one cell does over time.

## `steady_state_stats()`: the long-run summary

This one doesn't keep the film. It runs one very long simulation and only keeps a running tally. At the end it tells you:
- **mean**: the typical number of mRNAs and proteins
- **variance**: how spread out the numbers are
- **Fano factor**: variance ÷ mean, a single "how noisy is it" number. 1 means ordinary randomness, and above 1 means bursty.

Two details make the averages fair:
- **It ignores the start (`t_burn`).** The cell begins empty and takes a while to settle, and you don't want that warm-up in your average.
- **It weights by time.** If the cell sat at 5 proteins for 10 minutes and at 6 proteins for 1 second, the 5 should count far more. It multiplies each count by how long the cell stayed there.

**Use it when you want numbers to compare with theory.**

## How they relate

Both call the same hidden engine, `_simulate`, so they run the same simulation. The only difference is what they keep: `gillespie()` keeps snapshots, and `steady_state_stats()` keeps averages.

# Part 2: How to write a test yourself

Every test follows the same three steps:

1. **Pick a situation where you already know the right answer.** This is the hard, thinking part.
2. **Run the function** in that situation.
3. **Compare** what you got with what you expected, using `self.assert...`.

The simulation is random, so you'll almost never get the expected answer exactly. Two habits handle that:
- **Allow a margin:** `assertAlmostEqual(got, expected, delta=...)` passes if they're within `delta`.
- **Fix the seed:** `seed=1` makes the "random" numbers the same every run, so a test can't pass one day and fail the next.

## Where to find "known answers"

| Kind of known answer | Example |
|---|---|
| **Basic facts about the output** | `times` starts at 0 and ends at `t_max`; counts are never negative |
| **Extreme or simple cases** | If β = 0, no protein is ever made. If nothing can happen, the counts never change. |
| **Theory formulas** | Average mRNA = r / γm; mRNA Fano factor = 1 |

**Good habits with seeds in tests:**
1. **Always pass a seed in tests.** A test that passes one run and fails the next is very hard to debug, and a fixed seed makes every run identical.
2. **Still allow a margin.** A seed fixes which random numbers you get, but it doesn't make the answer exactly equal to theory. Keep using `delta=` when comparing to formulas.
3. **Don't pick margins to fit one lucky seed.** If a test only passes with `seed=7`, your margin is too tight. Try a few different seeds once by hand, as I did with 40 seeds for `test_gillespie_theory.py`, and make sure they all pass.
4. **Use different seeds for independent runs.** When you simulate many cells, give each one its own seed, such as `seed=SEED + i` in a loop. Using the same seed for every cell gives you the same cell copied many times.
5. **Leave the seed out outside of tests.** In your web app you probably want `seed=None`, so each click gives a fresh random cell.

One limitation: a seed makes results repeatable on the same setup. If numba is installed on one computer and not another, the code takes a different path for its random numbers, so the same seed can give different numbers. That's another reason to test against theory with margins, not against exact numbers you copied from one run.

## 2. What the chosen paper found, in plain language

The paper's question: if a cell makes a protein from a gene, how much does the protein count bounce around from cell to cell, and what controls that? It uses your two-step model: mRNA is made and destroyed, and each mRNA makes proteins before it's destroyed.

**Finding 1: Noise is set mainly at translation.**
Each mRNA makes a burst of proteins before it's destroyed, about b = β/γm proteins on average. Proteins arrive in clumps rather than one at a time, and bigger clumps mean more noise. The paper measures noise with the Fano factor (variance ÷ mean), which it calls "noise strength":

> Fano factor of protein = 1 + β/(γm + γp), which is about 1 + b when mRNA breaks down much faster than protein.

So a gene with efficient translation (big b) is noisy. A gene that reaches the same protein level with lots of transcription and little translation is quiet. Your `test_gillespie.py` already checks this formula.

**Finding 2: You can set the average and the noise separately.**
The average protein level is rβ/(γm·γp), and the noise depends mostly on b. A cell can raise transcription and lower translation by the same factor. The average stays the same and the noise drops. That's the paper's main message.

**Finding 3: Noise is highest just after the gene switches on.**
When a gene is first turned on, the noise briefly rises above its final value before settling. The abstract says the noise "immediately following single gene induction is almost twice the final steady-state value."

**Finding 4: Noise passes down a chain of genes.**
If protein A controls gene B, A's fluctuations make B noisy too. The authors suggest this is why genes for control proteins are often translated inefficiently: it keeps their noise low.

**Finding 5: Negative feedback reduces noise.**
If a protein represses its own gene, too much protein slows production and too little speeds it up. This pulls the count back toward the average, so the noise drops. This links Level 1 (a repressor) to your scientific question about how transcription factors affect protein production.

**About the noise measure:** a later review by van Oudenaarden's group says noise depends on transcription but not translation, which sounds like the opposite. It isn't a contradiction. They measure noise differently there, as the standard deviation divided by the mean, not the Fano factor. Use the Fano factor, as this paper does, and say so in your write-up.

## How to test parameter values yourself

Use `try_parameters.py`. You give it values in the paper's notation, and it converts them, simulates, and tells you whether the simulation matches the formula.

### Step 1: Find the values in a figure caption

For example, Figure 2's caption says "protein half-life is fixed at 1 h, b = 20, and k_R = 0.01 s⁻¹ … mRNA half-life of 120, 24". Put every time in **seconds**: 1 h = 3600 s and 2 min = 120 s.

### Step 2: Run it

```
python3 try_parameters.py --kR 0.01 --b 20 --mrna-half-life 120 --protein-half-life 3600
```

| Option | Paper's symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `--kR` | k_R | transcripts made per second |
| `--b` | b | proteins made per mRNA |
| `--mrna-half-life` | ln2/γ_R | seconds |
| `--protein-half-life` | ln2/γ_P | seconds |
| `--Kd 800 --n 2` | K_d, n | optional: add negative feedback (Figure 3) |
| `--induction` | | optional: also check the noise after switching on (Figure 2) |

### Step 3: Read the output

```
Your values, converted to gillespie.py arguments:
  r = kR = 0.01   gamma_m = ln2/120 = 0.0057762   beta = b*gamma_m = 0.11552   gamma_p = ...

Paper's prediction (Eq. 1): mean protein 1038.7, Fano factor 20.35

Simulation: 8 runs x 7.2e+06 s each. Steady-state check (pass = within 3 SE):
  mean protein   formula   1038.74   simulation   1034.93 ± 5.32   (-0.4%)   PASS
  Fano factor    formula     20.35   simulation     ...
```

The output has three parts:
1. **The conversion:** your numbers in the paper's notation become `gillespie()`'s arguments. Remember that `beta` = b × γ_m, not b itself.
2. **The prediction:** what the paper's formula says should happen.
3. **The check:** the simulation's average over several runs, ± its standard error. PASS means they agree within 3 standard errors.

### Step 4: If something fails, work through this in order

1. **Run longer** (`--length`). Short runs scatter a lot and lean low. My quick test with `--length 2e6` failed by 9.7%; with 5×10⁷ s it matched within 0.2%. The default is 2,000 protein half-lives, which is long enough.
2. **Try another seed** (`--seed 5`). At 3 standard errors, about 1 check in 100 fails just by chance.
3. **Check your units.** Minutes typed where seconds belong is the most common mistake.
4. **With feedback, it may be the formula.** For steep feedback, Eq. 3 itself is off by a few percent, as in Figure 3b.
